Elsewhere / Research + Plan

One white tee.
Two points of view.

This page turns the research into a small website plan. Elsewhere is for young creatives who use style to show their personality. This is an audience hypothesis, not a result from customer interviews.

Brand promise: Express your individuality through everyday clothing while thinking more carefully about your choices. The design keeps the selected Postmodernist direction, Sage + Creator archetypes, green/plum/lavender palette, and “Less ordinary. More you.”

Research checked November 16, 2026

What three shirt sites show

I reviewed the public product or collection pages below. These are observations of page content, not usability tests or proof of what shoppers prefer.

Website and source What I noticed What Elsewhere can use
Everlane — Women’s Tees The collection describes fabric and shape, then suggests ways to dress a tee up or down with other pieces. Show a few optional outfit ideas so visitors can picture their own style. Only make product claims Elsewhere can verify.
Gap — Classic T-Shirt The product page puts white color, fit, size choices, price, and Add to Bag near the product facts. Keep Elsewhere’s $15 illustrative price, sizes, and demo action clear and easy to find. Explain that checkout is only simulated.
UNIQLO — AIRism T-Shirt The product page connects the tee to specific comfort features and includes color, size, material, and care details. Useful facts make a basic easier to compare. Elsewhere must not copy fabric, performance, or sustainability claims that have not been verified for its own shirt.

Sources: official brand pages. Accessed November 16, 2026.

Two alternative brand positions

Same audience. Different emphasis.

Both ideas keep the same white T-shirt, price, audience hypothesis, promise, and brand identity. They test which part of the promise should lead on the page.

A · Details first

The thoughtful everyday essential

“A simple piece, made yours.” Start with the shirt’s known facts, size, and price, then offer styling ideas. Sage clarity leads; Creator expression stays in the page.

Risk: It could feel too ordinary unless the playful Elsewhere style remains visible.

B · Style first

The everyday canvas

“Less ordinary. More you.” Begin with expressive imagery and free styling prompts, then show the same shirt facts and purchase controls. Creator expression leads; Sage keeps the path clear.

Risk: Visitors could miss price or size details if the visuals take over.

B carries forward Elsewhere’s already selected Postmodernist direction. A remains the useful comparison. This choice follows the existing brand decision; it does not prove which version customers prefer.

Plan / compare two approaches

Hold the offer steady

Both versions use the same product, $15 illustrative price, sizes, headline, supporting sentence, image, styling prompt, and action. The comparison changes the information order and visual treatment.

Approach What appears first Visual direction Preview
A · Details first Product facts, $15 illustrative price, size, and quantity. Modernist structure; clean order and clear hierarchy. Open A
B · Style first Shirt image and personal-style context; facts stay available. Selected Postmodernist direction; expressive but readable. Open B

Check the same task in both: find the price, choose M, add 2 shirts, review the $30 demo total, and finish simulated checkout. Record the actual outcome in each version before deciding what to keep.

Four user stories and checks

What the first version must do

S1 / Compare

See the two layouts

As a young creative, I want to compare two ways to present the shirt so I can see how it could fit my style.

Check: Switch A and B; confirm the same price, headline, copy, image, sizes, and action remain, and the intended order changes.

S2 / Choose

Pick a size and quantity

As a shopper, I want the size and quantity I select to appear in my demo bag with a clear total.

Check: Choose M and quantity 2; confirm the bag shows M × 2 and $30. Missing size or a quantity outside 1–5 must not add an item.

S3 / Review

Finish a simulated checkout

As a visitor, I want to review the demo total and receive a reference so I know the example completed.

Check: Use a synthetic email; confirm the server-calculated amount and demo reference. No payment details or real purchase are requested.

S4 / Keep

Find the saved order again

As the project owner, I want a local order saved in SQLite so I can retrieve it after restarting the server.

Check: Save a demo order, stop and restart the same local server/database, then confirm the same reference, size, quantity, and total appear in history.

Two honest persuasion features

Helpful, optional, and clear

Reciprocity · included

Free styling ideas

Visitors can use the styling prompts with clothes they already own. The value is inspiration; there is no signup or extra purchase needed.

Commitment and consistency · added

Choose a style mood

A visitor may choose “relaxed,” “layered,” or “a little color” to get a matching free prompt. The choice is optional, can be changed, and creates no purchase pressure.

Local-only backend

A demo order, saved in SQLite

On the local Node server, a simulated order is validated and its item, size, quantity, and server-calculated total are saved to SQLite. The demo email is checked but not saved. The database lives outside the public files. There are no real payments or fulfilled orders.

The published GitHub Pages preview has no Node server. It stores demo history in the current browser only. The page labels these two modes so browser storage is not confused with SQLite.
